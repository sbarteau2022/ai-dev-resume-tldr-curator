01 — The letter
To the TLDR AI editorial team August 1, 2026

Dear TLDR AI team,

I read TLDR AI the way I hope its readers do — fast, and trusting that if something made the cut, it matters and it’s true. I’m writing to apply for the content role because the work I already do every day is the same work you publish: I build AI systems, then explain them clearly enough that someone who wasn’t in the room can follow the reasoning — and check it themselves.

Over the past year that has become seventeen repositories. A persistent, tool-using agent that reaches roughly a hundred tools and is auditable end to end. The memory geometry underneath it. A restaurant-finance platform that deliberately refuses to let a model do the arithmetic. A legal-statute retriever with zero citation drift. Every one ships with documentation written for a reader, not a compiler — which makes those READMEs, in effect, a year of AI explainers. And alongside the code is a parallel body of formal writing: seventy-six papers, publicly archived on PhilArchive, taking the ideas underneath these systems down to their proofs — work that has drawn 10,000+ downloads across six continents with no institution and no promotion behind it, on the strength of the writing alone. The enclosed sweep walks through the strongest of both.

The habit I’d bring to your inbox is the one an AI newsletter needs most: I report the losses. One of my research repos ran its own method against random and hub-shortcut controls, watched it lose on recall breadth, and put the losing table right in the README — then earned the result back under a harder, leakage-proof test. Never oversell; always show the number that undercuts you. That instinct is the entire basis of a reader’s trust, and it’s my default setting.

The rarest thing in AI writing isn’t enthusiasm. It’s a writer who’ll tell you the method lost to random shortcuts — and then show you the table.

I can usually tell your readers whether a new claim is real because I’ve shipped the neighboring thing, and I can compress it to ninety seconds without softening what’s true. That is exactly what I’d love to do for the people who read TLDR AI over their morning coffee. What follows is the evidence; I’d welcome the chance to talk it through.

02 — Core exemplars

Four pieces that carry the whole argument.

Chosen not because they’re the biggest, but because each proves a different thing an AI editor needs to trust: depth, honesty, real-world stakes, and the ability to make hard math legible.

i.

Elle — a persistent, tool-using mind you can audit

elle-worker · Elle · TypeScript · Cloudflare Workers

The depth credential. One Cloudflare Worker holds an entire agent: a ReAct loop that picks both a tool and a model tier per step, across ~109 tools — corpus RAG, live web research, a sandboxed run_code, a self-modifying “forge,” trading, and a memory graph. An autonomous conductor runs the same loop unprompted against standing goals.

The part worth writing about: security is one table. A caller’s scope (public → member → full) gates which tools even render into the prompt — so the model can never be tempted by a tool it isn’t allowed to call. That’s a genuinely publishable idea about agent safety, and the README explains it in a paragraph.

ii.

The negative result, published on purpose

Dynanic-Hyperbolic-Neural-Graph · TypeScript · 171 tests, zero runtime deps

The trust credential — the most important one for a newsletter. This repo embeds an LLM’s memory graph into two geometries at once (a hyperbolic Poincaré ball for depth, a flat torus for recurrence) and tests whether “wormhole” shortcuts help retrieval. It could have just claimed victory. Instead it ran controls — random shortcuts, hub shortcuts, a phase-permutation null — and reported the loss out loud:

“It does not beat random scattering on recall breadth (0.588 vs. 0.639)… Until [the real-ledger holdout] has been run, the synthetic results are a reason to try, not a reason to ship.”

Then it went further: a chronological holdout where the answer key lives strictly in the graph’s future, so leakage is structurally impossible — and there the method posts a 6.5× lift over random. That arc — honest loss, then a clean win under a harder test — is exactly how a credible AI newsletter should read a paper.

iii.

AI that refuses to do the arithmetic itself

RAPIDAi (“Atlas Analytics”) · TypeScript · Cloudflare D1 · R2 · Vectorize

The applied-stakes credential. A restaurant-finance platform where every invoice passes a two-gate pipeline — SHA-256 integrity, then line-by-line arithmetic reconciliation — before it touches the database. Questions are answered by ~20 deterministic SQL tools with returned provenance, not by asking a model to add up numbers.

This is the cleanest real-world illustration of a debate the AI field keeps having — when do you let the LLM compute, and when do you make it call a verified tool? — with a shipped answer and a reason. Perfect explainer material.

iv.

Making a proof run in ninety seconds

toroidal-isotropic-transformer · Python · one file, no dependencies

The legibility credential. A single python3 tit.py — no GPU, no setup — empirically confirms that the golden ratio φ is forced out of three plain conditions (treat all positions equally, suppress without preference, iterate). The README states the four predictions before the code so a reader can judge them independently, and even ties the result to Google’s ICLR-2026 TurboQuant constant.

This is the whole TLDR craft in miniature: take an intimidating idea, give the reader a runnable thing they can check themselves, and connect it to news they’ve actually heard of.

06 — The fit

What this body of work says about a content hire.

I actually build it

Agents, RAG, evaluation harnesses, retrieval, fine-grained tool-use — hands-on, in production on Cloudflare. When a paper drops, I can tell the readers whether the claim is real because I’ve shipped the neighboring thing.

I write for the reader

Seventeen READMEs that lead with the point, name things the way a person would, and cut jargon to length without softening it. That’s the TLDR format before it had a name.

I report the losses

Controls, nulls, honest caveats, “a reason to try, not a reason to ship.” An AI newsletter’s whole value is a reader trusting you didn’t oversell. My default is to show the table that undercuts my own claim.

The through-line across every repo is a single conviction: an intelligence is only as good as your ability to check it. That’s a builder’s principle and an editor’s principle at once — which is precisely why I want to write it for the people who read TLDR AI over their morning coffee.
